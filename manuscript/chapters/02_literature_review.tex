\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{chap:literature}

Probability of default is conditional on an event definition, a horizon, a risk set, and an information set. These elements determine the estimand. In a loan-month model, the relevant quantity is $\pi_{i,t}^{(12)}=\Pr(Y_{i,t}^{(12)}=1\mid\mathcal{F}_t)$, where the outcome records whether loan $i$ first reaches serious delinquency during the following twelve months. A change in the delinquency threshold, prediction horizon, treatment of terminated loans, or information available at month $t$ changes the probability being estimated. PD should therefore be interpreted as a forecast for an eligible loan at a defined date rather than as an unconditional borrower attribute.

This conditional interpretation separates PD estimation from the retrospective description of defaulted loans. A retrospective analysis asks which characteristics are common among realised defaults. A PD model asks how those characteristics alter the probability of a future event among loans that remain eligible to default. The risk-set restriction is part of that question. Including observations that are already seriously delinquent changes the task from transition prediction to state classification. Treating loans with incomplete follow-up as non-events changes the observed event frequency and can bias calibration. Loan-level PD estimation therefore requires the event construction and the eligible population to be stated together.

The horizon also affects the meaning of the probability. A one-month delinquency probability and a twelve-month serious-delinquency probability are not interchangeable. The longer horizon aggregates the chance of an event over several future months and generally produces a higher cumulative event rate. It also increases the importance of adequate follow-up. The twelve-month horizon used in this study is fixed for every loan-month, which permits a direct comparison between predicted values and realised frequencies. It does not estimate a lifetime default probability or a time-to-event hazard.

Logistic regression remains a central method for loan-level credit scoring. It maps a linear predictor to the unit interval and provides coefficients that describe changes in log-odds. Credit-scoring research has used logistic models for both origination characteristics and behavioural information observed after account opening \citep{hand_henley_1997,thomas2000survey,costa2020logistic}. Origination variables describe initial credit quality, leverage, balance, interest rate, term, and borrower structure. Monthly performance variables describe the subsequent payment path. Recent delinquency, its duration, and evidence of cure can contain information that is not available in the origination record.

The logistic link provides a coherent probability only when the fitted linear predictor is combined with an appropriate estimation and calibration procedure. The coefficient on a continuous variable describes a change in log-odds holding the other included variables constant. This conditional interpretation is useful, but it is sensitive to the set of correlated predictors and their scaling. A coefficient should not be read as a causal effect unless the design supports that claim. In a predictive PD model, its principal role is to make the contribution of a defined feature explicit.

Origination and behavioural variables serve different purposes. Origination characteristics measure the risk profile at the start of the loan. Monthly balance, interest rate, age, remaining term, and delinquency history update that profile as the account evolves. Behavioural variables can dominate short-horizon prediction because they record deterioration that has already begun. Their construction must stop at the prediction month. A rolling count that incorporates month $t+1$, even indirectly, would contaminate the information set used for a month-$t$ forecast.

Repeated loan-month observations create another distinction from a cross-sectional scorecard. The same loan contributes several observations, and its static characteristics recur while its age, balance, payment record, and common macroeconomic environment change. The resulting sample can be large without containing an equally large number of independent economic periods. Chronological validation is therefore more informative for temporal transport than a random division of loan-month rows. Random allocation would place observations from the same calendar month, and often the same loan, on both sides of the evaluation boundary.

A borrower-only logistic model is static in calendar time unless temporal terms are introduced explicitly. Its coefficients imply that a unit change in a borrower or loan characteristic has the same log-odds effect in all months. This is a strong restriction for mortgage PD. The risk associated with leverage, interest cost, or recent delinquency may differ when labour income, financing conditions, and collateral values deteriorate together. A static scorecard can retain useful cross-sectional discrimination while its probability levels drift as the economic environment changes.

Static coefficients can fail in two related ways. The intercept may no longer represent the prevailing base rate, and the slopes may no longer represent the prevailing relationship between loan characteristics and the outcome. Recalibrating the intercept can address a broad level shift. It cannot correct a change in the relative importance of credit score, leverage, interest cost, or payment history. A model that allows only an intercept adjustment remains static in its borrower sensitivities.

The distinction matters for evaluation. If every predicted probability is too high by a similar amount, the model can preserve ROC-AUC while producing poor Brier score and log loss. If the slopes change, ranking can also deteriorate. A complete assessment should therefore compare probability scores, calibration levels, and discrimination. A single measure cannot identify whether error arises from a base-rate shift, weak separation, or both.

Macroeconomic credit models address part of this problem by adding observed economic variables to the loan-level equation. Unemployment, interest rates, inflation, house prices, and related variables connect default forecasts to common conditions. Evidence from survival and stress-testing models shows that macroeconomic variables can improve the temporal responsiveness of retail credit models \citep{bellotti2009credit,bellotti2013stress}. The usual additive form shifts the log-odds by the same macroeconomic contribution for all loans. It does not imply that a loan characteristic becomes more or less consequential as the economy moves into stress. A large correlated macro vector can also weaken stability and interpretation.

An additive macroeconomic model has the form $\operatorname{logit}(\pi_{i,t})=\beta_0+X_{i,t}^{\top}\beta+M_t^{\top}\gamma$. The same $M_t^{\top}\gamma$ is applied to every loan observed in month $t$. Borrowers can still receive different PDs because their $X_{i,t}$ values differ, but the marginal log-odds effect of each borrower feature remains $\beta_j$ in every month. The macroeconomic vector changes the common level of risk rather than the cross-sectional sensitivity to borrower information.

This form is useful as a baseline because it tests whether direct macroeconomic measurements add information after loan-level variables are included. It is also demanding to estimate when several indicators describe related economic mechanisms. Policy rates, mortgage rates, Treasury yields, and spreads are correlated. Unemployment, claims, payrolls, income, and sentiment overlap in their description of labour and household conditions. Regularisation can stabilise their coefficients, but the fitted vector can remain difficult to summarise economically.

The timing of macroeconomic variables must match the information set. Levels, changes, spreads, and growth rates use different amounts of historical information. A twelve-month growth rate at month $t$ is available from observations at $t$ and $t-12$. A monthly change is available from $t$ and $t-1$. These backward-looking transformations are consistent with a month-$t$ forecast. Revisions to public macroeconomic series create a separate real-time issue when current-vintage data are used to describe earlier months. The present study does not reconstruct historical data vintages, so its macroeconomic inputs should be interpreted as revised series aligned by calendar month.

Hidden Markov models provide a parsimonious representation of persistent latent conditions. The observed macroeconomic vector is assumed to arise from one of a finite number of unobserved states, while the state follows a first-order Markov chain. State persistence distinguishes an HMM from a contemporaneous classification rule. The model can summarise several indicators as a probability distribution over economic states rather than assigning an observed month to a regime without uncertainty \citep{rabiner1989hmm,hamilton1989regimes}.

A two-state HMM separates the macroeconomic sequence into two fitted distributions. The state labels are statistical and have no inherent economic meaning. One state becomes Stress only after its fitted emission profile is compared with the orientation of the inputs. This post-estimation orientation avoids assigning meaning to the arbitrary numeric state labels. It also makes the definition reproducible: the stress state is the state with the larger combined mean across variables that have been oriented towards adverse conditions.

The transition matrix represents persistence. Large diagonal probabilities imply that the latent state tends to remain unchanged from one month to the next. This persistence can reduce month-to-month noise in the regime signal. It can also produce probabilities close to zero or one when the fitted state distributions are well separated. Such certainty is not automatically evidence of a superior economic signal. Near-degenerate probabilities may leave too little variation for interaction effects to be distinguished in a later test period.

The Gaussian emission assumption is a modelling restriction. Within each state, the factor vector is represented by a multivariate normal distribution with a state-specific mean and covariance matrix. Full covariance matrices allow the relationships among factors to differ by state. They require more parameters than diagonal covariance matrices and can be sensitive when the number of monthly observations is limited. Reducing the macroeconomic inputs to a small number of interpretable factors limits this parameter burden without claiming that the factors exhaust every dimension of the macroeconomy.

The distinction between filtered and smoothed state probabilities is essential in forecasting. A smoothed probability conditions on the complete observed sequence and therefore uses information after month $t$. A filtered probability conditions on observations through month $t$. The filtered stress probability, denoted $q_t=\Pr(R_t=\text{Stress}\mid Z_{1:t};\widehat\Theta)$, is the appropriate state output for a month-$t$ PD equation. It remains conditional on the method used to estimate $\widehat\Theta$. If HMM parameters and transformations are fitted over the full sample, forward filtering prevents direct use of future observations in the recursion but does not create a fully real-time estimate.

This distinction produces two separate information questions. The first concerns state inference given fixed parameters. Forward filtering answers that question using $Z_{1:t}$. The second concerns parameter estimation. A fully real-time design would estimate transformations, scaling parameters, and HMM parameters using information available at each forecast origin. Using a full-window estimate answers a narrower question: what filtered state path is implied by parameters fitted to the complete study window? The resulting signal is more defensible than a smoothed path for downstream prediction, but it should not be described as strictly out of sample.

The continuous probability $q_t$ contains more information than the hard indicator $\mathbb{I}(q_t\geq0.5)$. Values near 0.5 express state uncertainty. Values near zero or one express stronger assignment under the fitted model. A hard threshold discards this gradation and can create an artificial discontinuity. It is still useful for descriptive counts and diagnostic slices. It is unsuitable for a normal-versus-stress comparison when every observation in an evaluation period lies on the same side of the threshold.

Regime-dependent credit models allow default processes to vary across persistent economic states. Existing applications have examined cyclical rating migration and hidden-state default intensity \citep{bangia_et_al_2002,yu2018modeling}. This literature establishes that latent regimes can enter credit-risk models, but it does not settle whether an HMM improves a loan-level mortgage PD after borrower and payment-history information is included. It also leaves a choice between a hard regime indicator and a continuous state probability. A continuous probability preserves uncertainty and avoids an abrupt change at an arbitrary threshold.

Portfolio-level regime models and loan-level regime-aware models answer different questions. A portfolio model can allow a common transition matrix or default intensity to change with the state. A loan-level model must also represent cross-sectional heterogeneity among loans observed in the same month. The common state alone cannot distinguish two borrowers. Borrower information remains the primary source of cross-sectional variation, while the regime signal supplies a shared time-varying context.

This layered interpretation places the HMM between the macroeconomic data and the PD equation. It is a dimension-reduction and state-inference component rather than a replacement for the credit model. The HMM is estimated from monthly macroeconomic factors. The logistic model is estimated from loan-month outcomes. Their parameters have different meanings and are supported by different effective sample sizes. Millions of loan-months do not eliminate uncertainty in a regime process estimated from slightly more than a decade of monthly observations.

A regime-aware loan-level logistic model can include $q_t$ as a level effect and interact it with selected components of $X_{i,t}$. The log-odds contribution of feature $j$ then becomes $\beta_j+q_t\theta_j$. This structure is more flexible than an additive macroeconomic model and remains interpretable by construction. Interpretation still requires care. The sign and magnitude of a standardised interaction coefficient depend on the included main effects, feature scaling, and correlation among variables. The structure identifies which sensitivities are allowed to vary; it does not by itself establish a causal effect of the macroeconomic state.

The level term and interaction terms serve distinct purposes. The coefficient on $q_t$ shifts the log-odds for every loan after accounting for $X_{i,t}$. Each interaction changes the coefficient on one selected loan-level feature as $q_t$ changes. A stress-level-only model can therefore be compared with the interaction model. If the level-only model improves but the interaction model does not, the evidence favours a common macroeconomic shift. If the interaction model improves while the level-only model does not, the selected changing sensitivities are the more plausible source of the predictive difference.

Interaction selection should remain limited when interpretation is a central aim. Interacting every loan-level variable with every macroeconomic series would create a large model whose coefficients are difficult to separate. A smaller set based on credit quality, leverage, interest cost, and recent payment performance preserves the economic question. Regularisation remains necessary because the main effects and their products with a common $q_t$ can be strongly correlated, especially when $q_t$ spends long periods near an endpoint.

When $q_t$ has little variation in the test period, the regime-aware model can still generate different predictions because the interaction coefficients were estimated using earlier variation and because borrower features vary across loans. Its held-out performance can therefore be measured. What cannot be established is performance across distinct test regimes. A favourable pooled score during an all-stress period is evidence about that terminal period, not evidence of a stable normal-to-stress contrast.

Interpretability is relevant because a higher-capacity specification can improve fit while obscuring the source of a probability change. A regularised logistic model retains an explicit functional form and permits direct inspection of borrower--regime terms. This advantage does not exempt the model from empirical validation. A transparent model can still be unstable, poorly calibrated, or sensitive to the estimation window. Interpretability concerns the form of the model. Predictive validity concerns its behaviour on data not used for estimation.

Regularisation changes the interpretation of fitted coefficients. An $L_2$ penalty shrinks correlated coefficients towards zero but does not set most of them exactly to zero. The resulting model can be more stable than an unpenalised fit, particularly with macroeconomic and interaction terms. Coefficients remain conditional associations on the standardised scale. Their presence documents the modelled pathway from a predictor to log-odds; their magnitude should not be presented as an unpenalised structural parameter.

Interpretability also depends on reporting the complete specification. A label such as ``regime-aware'' is insufficient unless the main effects, regime input, and interaction set are defined. The calibration map is part of the final probability model because it changes probability levels after the logistic fit. A transparent account should therefore distinguish the raw logistic probability, the calibration transformation estimated on validation data, and the final probability used for evaluation.

Calibration and discrimination answer different questions. ROC-AUC measures the ordering of cases and controls, while PR-AUC focuses more directly on positive-class retrieval when defaults are rare \citep{davis_goadrich_2006,saito2015prroc}. Neither measure assesses whether predicted probabilities match observed frequencies. The Brier score and log loss are proper scoring rules that assess probability forecasts \citep{brier1950,gneiting_raftery_2007}. Calibration curves compare predicted and observed rates across probability groups. A Brier decomposition separates reliability, resolution, and outcome uncertainty, although its numerical values depend on the grouping rule.

The Brier score averages squared probability errors. It is sensitive to calibration and discrimination but is often dominated by the large number of non-events when the outcome is rare. Log loss gives a larger penalty to confident errors because it is logarithmic. The two scores can rank models differently. Agreement between them strengthens a comparison, while disagreement requires a metric-specific interpretation. Neither score should be converted into a claim of economic significance without considering the size of the difference and the dependence among repeated observations.

ROC-AUC is invariant to monotonic recalibration. A model can retain the same ROC-AUC after its probabilities are shifted substantially. PR-AUC is also primarily a ranking measure, but its scale depends on event prevalence. Reporting the event rate makes its magnitude interpretable. In a rare-event setting, PR-AUC can distinguish models whose ROC curves appear similar because it places more emphasis on the precision of high-ranked positive predictions.

Calibration has several levels. Mean calibration compares the average predicted PD with the overall event rate. Calibration-in-the-large can be poor even when a curve has acceptable local shape. A calibration slope assesses whether probabilities are too dispersed or too compressed. Grouped curves show how predicted and observed rates compare across selected bins. These summaries complement one another. A model can have a lower Brier score than a comparator while both models materially overpredict the mean event rate.

The Murphy decomposition expresses a grouped Brier score as reliability minus resolution plus uncertainty. Reliability is smaller when average predictions within bins are close to observed rates. Resolution is larger when observed rates differ across bins. Uncertainty depends only on the overall event rate. Because the decomposition uses bins, it approximates rather than reproduces the ungrouped Brier score. Its components should be used to explain a result, not to replace the primary ungrouped score.

Post-estimation calibration can correct systematic distortion in raw probabilities. Platt scaling estimates an intercept and slope on the logit of the raw probability. Intercept-only recalibration adjusts the level while preserving the slope. Isotonic regression estimates a non-decreasing mapping with greater flexibility. These methods should be fitted without using the test outcomes. Chronological validation is therefore important for both model selection and calibration. It also reveals whether a result is stable across time rather than a consequence of random mixing across economic periods.

Platt scaling can correct both a broad level shift and excessive or insufficient dispersion on the logit scale. Intercept-only recalibration is more restrictive and leaves ranking and spread unchanged. Isotonic regression makes fewer shape assumptions but can adapt closely to the validation sample. Comparing these mappings on the same held-out period provides a sensitivity analysis. Choosing a new primary method after inspecting test outcomes would weaken the separation between validation and evaluation.

Temporal validation carries information beyond a single test score. A model that is best on validation and worse on test may have deteriorated as the population or economy changed. A model that is worse on validation and better on test may be suited to the terminal period without demonstrating stable superiority. Both patterns should be reported. The final interpretation should distinguish a held-out result from a recurring result across evaluation windows.

Portfolio calibration adds another level of assessment. Summing loan-level PDs within a month gives an expected number of defaults under the model. Comparing that quantity with the observed monthly count tests whether pooled probability performance translates into useful timing and level estimates. A model can improve the average Brier score by reallocating risk across loans while still overstating the total number of defaults. This is not a contradiction. It reflects different aggregations of the same probability forecasts.

The evidence supports a focused empirical comparison. Borrower-only logistic regression provides the loan-level benchmark. An additive macroeconomic logit tests whether observed macro variables shift PD levels. A regime-aware logit tests whether a filtered stress probability and selected borrower--regime interactions improve probability forecasts. The comparison should place calibration and proper scores alongside discrimination. It should also examine monthly predicted-default counts because a model can improve pooled loan-level scores while misstating the timing or level of portfolio risk.

This comparison gives each model a defined role. The borrower-only model establishes the value of cross-sectional and behavioural information without common economic inputs. The macro-augmented model tests a direct additive treatment of observed conditions. The regime-aware model tests a compressed latent-state signal and changing sensitivities. Diagnostic variants can separate the stress level from its interactions and assess whether the full macro vector contributes beyond the regime probability. The empirical conclusion should follow the joint pattern of these comparisons rather than the label attached to any one specification.
