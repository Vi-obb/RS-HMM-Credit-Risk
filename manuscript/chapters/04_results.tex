\section{Sample and Outcome}

The eligible panel contained 15,461,471 loan-months from 438,713 loans across the 2015--2024 cohorts. It contained 167,056 twelve-month transitions to 90 or more days past due, an event rate of 1.0805\%. The training sample contained 8,179,648 observations with an event rate of 1.5191\%. The validation sample contained 3,175,548 observations with an event rate of 0.4982\%. The test sample contained 4,106,275 observations and 26,983 events, an event rate of 0.6571\%. The variation in event rates across the chronological samples shows that the probability level changed materially over time.

Table~\ref{tab:cohort-coverage} reports the retained cohort coverage. Earlier cohorts contributed longer histories and more loan-months. The 2023 and 2024 cohorts contributed fewer observations because the model-ready data ended in September 2024 and each included loan-month required twelve subsequent observed months. Cohort event rates were not monotonic. They ranged from 0.4361\% for the 2020 cohort to 2.0893\% for the 2019 cohort.

The cohort table describes origination groups, while the training, validation, and test samples were divided by observation month. A loan from an early cohort could therefore contribute to more than one chronological split as it aged. No future outcome for a test-period loan-month entered model selection or probability calibration. The lower validation event rate and later increase in the test rate created a direct test of temporal calibration transfer.

\section{Estimated Macroeconomic Regimes}

The HMM used 129 monthly factor observations from January 2014 to September 2024. The fitted stress probability ranged from approximately $1.63\times10^{-102}$ to a value numerically equal to one over that window. Ninety-nine months were below the hard-state threshold of 0.5 and 30 months were above it. Figure~\ref{fig:hmm-stress-path} shows the filtered probability path.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{../reports/figures/hmm_stress_probability.png}
    \caption{Forward-filtered probability of the HMM stress state, January 2014--September 2024. Factor transformations and HMM parameters were estimated over the full displayed window.}
    \label{fig:hmm-stress-path}
\end{figure}

The probability was nearly saturated during the test period. It ranged from 0.999903 to a value numerically indistinguishable from one, and every test observation had $q_t\geq0.5$. The test data therefore did not contain a hard normal-regime comparison. Quantile bins separated only small numerical differences near one. The retained HMM answered the first research question by assigning a persistent stress state throughout the terminal test period, but it provided weak variation for estimating cross-regime test performance.

\section{Validation Results}

The borrower-only model performed best on the validation sample under the primary Platt calibration. Its Brier score was 0.004645 and its log loss was 0.026122. The regime-aware values were 0.004682 and 0.027021. The macro-augmented values were 0.004683 and 0.026850. Borrower-only also had the highest validation ROC-AUC, 0.798634, and PR-AUC, 0.114809. The corresponding regime-aware values were 0.767182 and 0.105560. The validation evidence did not show an advantage for the regime-aware specification.

The stress-level-only model had lower validation log loss than the regime-aware model but a slightly higher Brier score. The regime-aware plus macro model was weakest on validation Brier and log loss. The dynamic-stress model did not improve on borrower-only. The ranking therefore did not support adding complexity on the basis of the validation period.

Isotonic calibration reduced the validation Brier score to 0.004629 for borrower-only and 0.004647 for regime-aware. These values did not change the validation ordering. Platt scaling remained the primary calibration method specified in the implementation.

The regularisation choices also differed. Borrower-only, macro-augmented, stress-level-only, regime-aware plus macro, and dynamic stress selected $3\times10^{-5}$. Regime-aware selected $3\times10^{-6}$. These values were selected from the same candidate set using deterministic training and validation samples. The later test ranking was not used to choose them.

\section{Held-Out Test Results}

Table~\ref{tab:test-metrics} reports the primary Platt-calibrated test results. The regime-aware model had the lowest Brier score and log loss and the highest ROC-AUC and PR-AUC. Relative to borrower-only, its Brier score fell from 0.006055 to 0.006015, a reduction of 0.66\%. Its log loss fell from 0.033106 to 0.032945, a reduction of 0.48\%. ROC-AUC increased by 0.005276 and PR-AUC increased by 0.011736.

The nested specifications qualified this result. Adding $q_t$ without interactions produced a Brier score of 0.006089 and log loss of 0.034349. Adding the full macro vector to the regime-aware model produced 0.006105 and 0.034187. The dynamic-stress model substantially underpredicted the test event rate and performed worst on all four reported measures. The held-out advantage was specific to the selected regime-aware interaction structure.

The test ranking differed from the validation ranking. This limits the strength of the conclusion. The evidence supports a modest terminal-period improvement in pooled loan-level performance. It does not show that the improvement was stable across the validation and test periods.

The absolute test differences were small relative to the scale of the scores. The Brier difference between regime-aware and borrower-only was 0.0000397. The log-loss difference was 0.0001603. The report tables do not contain uncertainty intervals for these paired differences. The observed ordering is exact for the retained predictions, but its sampling uncertainty was not estimated.

\section{Calibration}

The test event rate was 0.006571. The mean Platt-calibrated PD was 0.010747 for borrower-only, 0.009743 for macro-augmented, and 0.010802 for regime-aware. Each core model overpredicted the mean event rate. Figure~\ref{fig:test-calibration} shows that validation-based calibration did not remove this temporal shift.

The ten-bin Brier decomposition gave reliability of 0.0000293 and resolution of 0.0001017 for the regime-aware model. Borrower-only had reliability of 0.0000549 and resolution of 0.0001006. The macro-augmented model had lower grouped reliability, 0.0000247, but worse ungrouped Brier score and discrimination. The grouped decomposition did not reproduce the complete model ordering.

\section{Temporal, Portfolio, and Discrimination Diagnostics}

The regime-aware model had a lower Brier score than borrower-only in both test-year groups. In 2023, the scores were 0.005432 and 0.005445. In 2024, they were 0.006546 and 0.006609. Both groups belonged to the same hard-stress classification and therefore did not constitute separate regime tests.

Monthly count calibration favoured borrower-only. Across the 18 test months, its mean absolute predicted-default-count error was 952.5, compared with 965.3 for regime-aware and 1,212.3 for macro-augmented. Mean absolute relative error was 0.6097 for borrower-only, 0.6367 for regime-aware, and 0.9016 for macro-augmented. The pooled Brier improvement did not translate into a lower average monthly count error.

Mean predicted PD exceeded the event rate for the three core models, so the positive monthly count errors were consistent with the pooled mean-calibration result. Borrower-only produced the smallest average error even though regime-aware allocated risk more effectively across individual loan-months according to the pooled Brier and ranking measures. This difference separates cross-sectional allocation from portfolio-level calibration.

PR-AUC showed the same ordering. This indicates better held-out ranking for the regime-aware model in the terminal period. It does not resolve the mean overprediction or the weaker validation results.

\section{Overall Interpretation}

The regime-aware model answered the second research question through an explicit $q_t$ level effect and eight borrower--regime interactions. The retained specification remained a regularised logistic model and did not include the raw macroeconomic vector. Its form allowed selected borrower sensitivities to change with the filtered stress probability.

For the third research question, the regime-aware model performed best on the pooled test Brier score, log loss, ROC-AUC, and PR-AUC. Borrower-only performed best on the corresponding validation measures and on monthly test default-count error. The macro-augmented baseline did not improve on borrower-only in the held-out test. The evidence is therefore mixed. It supports a modest test-period advantage for the regime-aware interaction model, subject to temporal instability and incomplete mean calibration.

The empirical design did not include rolling-origin HMM re-estimation or uncertainty intervals for differences between models. The test period also contained no hard-normal months. These limitations prevent a claim that the observed differences generalise across macroeconomic regimes or estimation windows.

The full-window HMM limitation and the test-period saturation are related but distinct. Full-window estimation affects the information available when the factor transformations and HMM parameters were fitted. Saturation affects the amount of variation in the resulting $q_t$ during evaluation. Forward filtering addressed future factor observations in the state recursion. It did not address either parameter re-estimation at each forecast origin or the lack of a hard-normal test period.

The absence of resampled uncertainty is also material. Loan-month observations from the same loan are dependent, and all loans in a calendar month share the same macroeconomic input. A conventional interval that treated 4,106,275 test rows as independent would therefore be misleading. The retained artefacts support exact point comparisons only. The results distinguish their numerical evidence from broader claims about repeated samples or other economic periods.
